5  Blinding

Blinding is the practice of withholding knowledge of treatment assignment from one or more parties in a clinical trial. While randomization (see Chapter 4) protects against bias at the point of allocation, blinding protects against bias after allocation, since how participants report symptoms, how investigators manage and assess them, and how data are handled and analysed can all be influenced, consciously or not, by knowing who receives which treatment (International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use 1998). Bias can arise when participants or investigators behave differently depending on the assigned treatment, for instance through more concomitant medication or closer follow-up. Bias can also arise when outcome assessors judge subjective endpoints more favourably in the group they expect to perform better. Bias can also arise when participants who suspect they receive placebo over-report a lack of effect, or if knowledge of assignment drives differential withdrawal, which creates missing data (see Chapter 14) patterns.

The more subjective the endpoint, the more blinding matters.

How far this protection extends depends on how many parties are kept unaware of the assignment. In a single-blind trial only the participant is blinded. In a double-blind trial both the participant and the investigator and site staff are blinded (default for confirmatory trials whenever it can be achieved (International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use 1998)). A triple-blind trial goes further and keeps those assessing outcomes, the sponsor team, or the statistician analysing the data blinded as well. Deciding who is blinded is only the intention; keeping them blinded requires practical machinery. Matching placebos must be identical in appearance, taste, and administration schedule, and when two active treatments cannot be made to look alike a double-dummy design is used, in which each participant receives one active and one placebo formulation. Any data that could reveal assignment indirectly, such as characteristic laboratory changes or well-known side effects, has to be handled centrally so that it does not undo the blind through the back door.

Blinding can be put under strain by interim analyses (see Chapter 13), the point in a trial where someone must look at accumulating data by treatment arm. The key distinction is between blinded and unblinded interim procedures. Blinded, non-comparative interims use pooled data without any knowledge of treatment assignment, as in blinded sample size re-estimation based on the overall event rate or the pooled variance, and because no comparative information is revealed they generally do not inflate the type I error or compromise trial integrity. Unblinded, comparative interims are a different matter. Group-sequential efficacy or futility looks require access to treatment-level results, and to protect the trial that access is confined to an independent Data Monitoring Committee supported by an independent statistician, separated from the sponsor and site personnel by a strict firewall. The sponsor team responsible for trial conduct should see at most the committee’s recommendation to continue, modify, or stop, and never the interim treatment effect itself, because even seemingly innocuous leakage matters: learning only that the trial has passed a futility boundary still conveys information about the effect size and can bias subsequent conduct. Operational discipline therefore matters as much as the statistics, since interim results that leak can change investigator behaviour, recruitment patterns, and endpoint assessment for the remainder of the trial, undermining precisely what blinding was meant to protect.

5.1 Open-Label Trial

A clinical trial can also be run as an open-label trial, in which all parties are aware of the treatment assignment. This is sometimes necessary when blinding is impossible or unethical, for instance when comparing a surgical intervention to a drug, or when the treatment has a distinctive administration schedule or side effect profile that cannot be masked. In such cases, other measures must be taken to mitigate bias, such as using objective endpoints, independent outcome adjudication, and standardized protocols for patient management. Open-label trials are often used in early-phase clinical trials (see Chapter 2) to assess safety and tolerability, or in long-term extension studies where blinding is impractical.

Even with these safeguards, the residual weakness of open-label trials is behavioural. Differential withdrawal, differential use of concomitant therapy, and expectation effects on patient-reported outcomes cannot be fully designed away, and they should be anticipated in the estimand discussion and probed through the sensitivity analyses (see Chapter 15).

5.2 Unblinding

Provisions for emergency unblinding of individual participants must be in place throughout the trial, typically for occasions when an adverse event requires knowledge of the treatment for clinical management. Such unblinding should be documented, limited to the individual participant, and should not reveal assignment to trial staff more broadly than necessary. At the end of the trial, the blind is formally broken only after database lock and, ideally, after the analysis population classifications (see Chapter 10) and the SAP have been finalized, so that no analytical decision can be influenced by knowledge of the treatment results.
